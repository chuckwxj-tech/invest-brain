**Morgan Stanley Raises Hyperscaler AI Capex Forecast: Repricing Opportunities in the Networking Layer and Calibration of Stock-Picking Logic**

On July 14, 2026, Morgan Stanley released a research report raising its 2027 capex forecast for the five major hyperscaler platforms — Meta, Amazon, Alphabet, Microsoft, and SpaceX — by 9% to approximately $1.2 trillion, with a further 10% increase to about $1.4 trillion in 2028. In its earlier mid‑2026 outlook, the firm had projected that U.S. large‑cap hyperscale cloud companies would spend $805 billion in 2026 (nearly double the $433 billion forecast a year earlier), with spending exceeding $1.1 trillion in 2027 and approaching $1.3 trillion in 2028. The article's claim that "2027 will reach $1.3 trillion" deviates from the latest July figures [**inconsistent with public information**]; the baseline should be approximately $1.2 trillion for 2027. The key drivers behind Morgan Stanley's upward revision are: a roughly 20% increase in GPU hardware costs, the data‑center construction cycle extending from 2 years to 3 years, and global supercomputing capacity expanding from 30GW in 2025 to 120GW in 2028 (quadrupling in four years) – supply‑chain bottlenecks and rising compute demand are jointly accelerating hyperscaler build‑out. The article's core insight – "a significant portion of this spending will not go to GPUs at all" – is supported by industry breakdowns: AI cluster networking interconnect costs account for approximately 15% of total costs under a three‑tier network architecture and 18% under a four‑tier architecture; optical transceivers alone account for about 60% of the networking bill. Optical communications' share of AI infrastructure capex has risen from the traditional ~5% to the 15–20% range, with optical modules ranking third in per‑server hardware costs, behind only GPUs and HBM. However, the article's physical estimate that "in a 200,000‑GPU cluster with total power of 435MW, optical transceivers consume about 17MW" appears only in a personal LinkedIn technical breakdown and Chinese secondary sources [**unverified**] – this should be treated as a rough order‑of‑magnitude reference, not authoritative fact.

The five beneficiary stocks selected in the article – Applied Optoelectronics (AAOI), Marvell Technology (MRVL), Credo Technology (CRDO), Lumentum Holdings (LUMENTUM), and Ciena (CIEN) – are generally accurately positioned within the AI cluster networking layer. However, their specific roles, elasticity, valuations, and risk‑reward profiles differ significantly, which the article does not differentiate. Applied Optoelectronics, as a small‑cap pure‑play optical module name, directly benefits from the surge in 800G/1.6T optical module demand, but its small size, customer concentration, and earnings volatility make it a high‑risk, high‑reward "pure transceiver exposure." Marvell, with its PAM4 DSP, custom ASICs, and optical interconnect IP, sits at the core value layer within transceivers, forming a "custom silicon duopoly" alongside Broadcom – but faces competition from Broadcom and in‑house ASIC development. Credo's active electrical cables (AEC) and SerDes technology offer differentiated advantages in the transition layer "before optical switching takes over distance extension," making it a key supplier for high‑speed interconnects both inside and outside AI server cabinets. Lumentum, as a well‑established optical component and module leader, benefits from the upgrade cycle from lower speeds to 800G/1.6T and CPO, but faces share competition from rivals such as Coherent. Ciena's strength lies in long‑haul DCI (data‑center interconnect) optical networking equipment, which is less directly tied to the "intra‑cluster interconnect" theme than the other four, benefiting more from cross‑campus AI infrastructure connectivity. The article presents these companies in parallel without providing each one's AI/data‑center revenue mix, recent earnings validation, valuation levels, or target price ranges – a typical limitation of stock‑picking notes: they offer "direction" but not "weighting" or "timing."

On the investment‑logic front, the article's judgment that "the photonics/networking group has fallen sharply with the semiconductor pullback over the past month, and is undervalued relative to its importance" is directionally valid but requires calibration. The Philadelphia Semiconductor Index pulling back over 20% from its highs, with optical module and networking sub‑sectors under pressure, is indeed factual, but "undervalued" is a relative judgment, not an absolute pricing conclusion – one must examine current forward P/E ratios, consensus 2026–2027 revenue growth estimates, gross‑margin trends, and free‑cash‑flow quality for these names. A more critical blind spot is that the repricing of networking capex share from 5% to 15–20% does not automatically translate linearly into EPS upgrades for each of these stocks, because: (1) competitive dynamics vary – the optical module space already features multiple players including ZJXT (Zhongji Innolight), Eoptolink, Coherent, and Lumentum; PAM4 DSP is a Marvell‑Broadcom duopoly; AEC is contested between Credo and Astera Labs; (2) technological uncertainty exists – next‑generation optical interconnect technologies such as CPO (co‑packaged optics), NPO, and OCS could reshape value‑chain distribution, with winners and losers yet to be determined; (3) Morgan Stanley's capex upgrade is fundamentally a "total‑amount increase" rather than a "networking‑share increase" – whether networking receives a proportional share of the budget depends on hyperscaler architectural choices and supplier bargaining power. The article's direct equation of "Morgan Stanley's capex forecast increase" with "these five stocks will inevitably benefit" is a correlation‑based inference, not a causal law – each company's quarterly earnings and order visibility must be independently verified in decision‑making.

**Viewpoint**:

This stock‑picking note exhibits a three‑layer credibility profile: "macro capex upgrade 100% verifiable, networking‑layer importance logic internally coherent, but individual stock selection requires independent validation." The macro facts are complete and authoritative: Morgan Stanley's July 14 research report, confirmed by multiple sources including BlockBeats, Tonghuashun Finance, and Caijing, raises 2027 capex for the five major hyperscalers by 9% to approximately $1.2 trillion and 2028 by 10% to about $1.4 trillion, with analyst Brian Nowak explicitly citing supply‑chain bottlenecks, roughly 20% GPU cost increases, and the data‑center construction cycle lengthening to three years as core drivers. However, the article's headline claim that "2027 will reach $1.3 trillion" deviates by about 8% from the latest July figure of $1.2 trillion [**inconsistent with public information**] – Morgan Stanley's May mid‑year outlook had previously projected 2028 "approaching $1.3 trillion," suggesting the article may have confused the year. For formal use, the baseline should be approximately $1.2 trillion (2027) / $1.4 trillion (2028); this does not affect the core judgment that "AI capex remains on an upward trajectory and has not peaked," but the numerical precision requires correction.

The logic of networking‑layer revaluation is supported by multiple industry sources. Industry breakdowns show AI cluster networking interconnect costs accounting for about 15% under a three‑tier architecture and 18% under four‑tier; optical transceivers make up about 60% of the networking bill. Optical communications' share of AI infrastructure capex has risen from the traditional 5% to the 15–20% range, a conclusion cross‑verified by Akash Borate's technical analysis, Mingde Dongjian industry research, and Zhongjin Online sector tracking. More crucially, optical modules exhibit a super‑linear relationship with GPU demand – the GPU‑to‑optical‑module ratio has risen from 1:2 to 1:3 to 1:5 – which is the structural reason why networking‑layer elasticity exceeds that of the compute layer. However, the article's physical estimate that "in a 200,000‑GPU cluster with total power of 435MW, optical transceivers consume about 17MW" appears only in a personal LinkedIn technical post and Chinese secondary sources [**unverified**], lacking authority and should be downgraded to a rough order‑of‑magnitude reference.

The report's main value lies in identifying the "third‑layer repricing" of AI infrastructure investment logic – shifting market attention from GPUs (first layer) and HBM/memory (second layer) toward networking interconnect and optical communications (third layer). This framework aligns with the industry consensus that "AI has entered the era of systems engineering, with bottlenecks shifting from chips to interconnect." However, as a stock‑picking note, the report has three notable limitations that decision‑makers should be aware of.

First, while the business positioning of the five stocks is broadly accurate, their risk‑reward profiles are not differentiated. Applied Optoelectronics is a small‑cap pure‑play optical module name with the greatest elasticity but also the highest earnings volatility and customer concentration risk. Marvell and Broadcom form a duopoly in PAM4 DSP and custom ASICs, but face intense competition from Broadcom and the threat of in‑house ASIC development by hyperscalers. Credo's AEC technology offers a differentiated window during the "copper‑to‑optical" transition, but if the technology pathway jumps directly to CPO, Credo's AEC could face premature obsolescence. Lumentum is a well‑established optical component leader benefiting from the speed‑upgrade cycle but faces intense share competition. Ciena's strength in long‑haul DCI makes it less directly tied to the "intra‑cluster interconnect" theme. Recommending these five stocks in parallel without providing weightings, valuation anchors, or price targets is a typical simplification of stock‑picking notes.

From an aggressive expert perspective: the article's judgment that "the networking layer is undervalued" is valid; the Philadelphia Semiconductor Index's over‑20% pullback offers a buying window; the 800G‑to‑1.6T upgrade, the accelerated CPO production timeline, and the continued upward revision of the GPU‑to‑optical‑module ratio constitute multiple catalysts over the next 4–6 quarters. Applied Optoelectronics and Credo, as small‑cap high‑beta names, could offer 3–5× upside in a capex‑upgrade cycle. From a neutral expert perspective: the repricing of networking's share from 5% to 15–20% is a structural trend, but each company must be validated individually – focus on quarterly data‑center/AI revenue mix, gross‑margin trends, and order visibility from major customers. Currently, more attention should be paid to the share battle between Marvell and Broadcom in custom silicon, and to the 1.6T optical module production cadence of Lumentum versus Coherent. From a conservative expert perspective: directly equating "Morgan Stanley's total capex increase" with "these five networking stocks will necessarily benefit" mistakes correlation for causation – competitive dynamics within networking, technological shifts (CPO/NPO/OCS), and hyperscaler self‑development of optical components are three variables that could change value distribution. Moreover, most of these stocks already embed high growth expectations; if Q3 2026 earnings show AI/data‑center revenue growth below market expectations, the pullback could exceed the broader market.

Four key risks require examination. First, competition dilution risk: the optical module space already features multiple competitors including ZJXT, Eoptolink, Coherent, and Lumentum; PAM4 DSP is a Marvell‑Broadcom duopoly; AEC is contested between Credo and Astera Labs – a total capex increase does not mean each participant benefits proportionally, and share shifts could leave some companies with "more volume but not more profit." Second, technology‑jump risk: next‑generation optical interconnect technologies such as CPO, NPO, and OCS are advancing in parallel; if hyperscalers leap directly from pluggable optics to CPO, companies currently championing 1.6T pluggable modules (Lumentum, Applied Optoelectronics) could face technological obsolescence, while Marvell's PAM4 DSP and Credo's SerDes IP may see value redistribution in the CPO era. Third, valuation and earnings‑validation risk: most of these five stocks have already accumulated significant gains in 2026, with forward P/Es at historical highs; each quarter's earnings must continue to validate AI/data‑center revenue growth – any signs of major customer order delays, gross‑margin misses, or capex execution slowdowns could trigger sector‑wide corrections. Fourth, macro capex execution risk: Morgan Stanley's upward revision assumes "persistent supply bottlenecks and compute demand"; if 2027 sees weaker‑than‑expected AI inference commercialization or tighter hyperscaler capex discipline (similar to Meta's 2024 capex pullback), networking names, as second‑order beneficiaries of capex, could see sharper pullbacks than front‑line GPU names.

In terms of decision‑making implications: For equity investors, "networking‑layer repricing" should be treated as a 2‑3 year industry theme, not a short‑term trading signal. In portfolio construction, a tiered approach is needed: Marvell, as a "custom silicon + PAM4" dual‑core player, offers relatively certain participation and stronger valuation absorption capacity, suitable as a core holding; Credo's AEC technology offers differentiation during the transition period, but CPO‑jump risk warrants caution, making it suitable for tactical positioning; Applied Optoelectronics, as a small‑cap pure exposure, offers both high elasticity and high risk, suitable for small‑sized tactical bets; Lumentum requires tracking of 1.6T volume share and CPO roadmap; Ciena has lower correlation with the intra‑cluster theme, more an independent DCI long‑haul story. The true observation variables are: quarterly capex execution rates of the five hyperscalers, 800G/1.6T optical module shipments, changes in the GPU‑to‑optical‑module ratio, and the CPO production timeline – these four variables will determine whether networking revaluation is a tactical opportunity or a 2‑3 year industry theme.

For supply‑chain investors, Morgan Stanley's capex upgrade to $1.2 trillion in 2027 and $1.4 trillion in 2028, with networking share repricing toward 15–20%, directly benefits optical modules, optical components, PAM4 DSP, SerDes IP, AEC, optical networking equipment, MPO/MTP pre‑terminated fiber, and future CPO packaging supply chains. However, technology‑jump risk must be monitored – in a scenario where the CPO production timeline accelerates, the value distribution of the traditional pluggable optical module supply chain could be reshaped, while companies with DSP, SerDes, and advanced packaging capabilities offer greater defensiveness during technology migration.

Overall, this note is a high‑quality forward‑looking framework for identifying the "third‑layer repricing of AI infrastructure investment logic." Morgan Stanley's capex upgrade is 100% verifiable, and the networking‑layer importance logic is cross‑corroborated. The report's greatest contribution is shifting market attention from GPUs alone toward the full infrastructure chain of "GPU + HBM + networking," identifying the super‑linear relationship between optical modules and GPU demand. Its greatest limitations are: recommending five stocks in parallel without differentiating risk‑reward profiles, failing to provide valuation anchors or earnings‑validation milestones, citing physical estimates like "17MW in a 200,000‑GPU cluster" from non‑authoritative sources, and directly linking the total capex increase to individual stock benefits – a correlation‑as‑causation risk. Investors using this framework should treat "networking‑layer repricing" as an industry theme, but independently validate each company's data‑center revenue mix, gross‑margin trends, order visibility from major customers, and technology‑pathway risk – these four dimensions will determine whether specific names capture a proportional share of the $1.2 trillion capex pie. This report is best suited as a qualitative framework for "AI networking‑layer investment logic"; specific stock‑selection decisions should still be dynamically calibrated against each company's quarterly earnings, 800G/1.6T shipment data, CPO production cadence, and changes in the GPU‑to‑optical‑module ratio.
