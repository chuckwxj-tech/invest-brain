## 原文

Last night $CBRS told you exactly what Applied Materials sells. It did it by listing the three things it refuses to buy.

$AMAT reports after the close tonight.

From Cerebras's Q2 release, 24 hours ago:

"Through our wafer-scale architecture, we avoid many of the components that are currently in short supply. We do not use HBM memory, CoWoS packaging or 3nm fabrication technology, all of which are currently supply limited."

Now Applied Materials' own guidance for calendar 2026:

Leading-edge foundry-logic, DRAM and advanced packaging will drive more than 80% of year-on-year WFE growth, with a similar profile in 2027. Semiconductor equipment revenue up more than 30%. Packaging up more than 50%.

Those are the same three sentences read from opposite ends of the chain.

One company's list of bottlenecks it escaped is the other company's revenue.

1. Every layer of this supply chain is currently shouting "the constraint is here."

Just from filings and calls in the last 72 hours:

$CBRS — "Data center space continues to be the bottleneck for the entire industry, and we are no exception."
$COHR — "We're not constrained in the assembly and test capacity right now. We're really just constrained by the ramp of the indium phosphide production."
$LITE — pump lasers "effectively sold out for the foreseeable future" despite rapid capacity expansion.
$AXTI — InP substrate GAAP gross margin 8.0% → 44.9% year over year.

Every one of those constraints resolves the same way: someone buys more equipment. That's the whole thesis in one line. Applied doesn't sell into the bottleneck. It sells the ability to widen it.

2. Tonight's first trap is in the guidance itself.

Applied guided Q3 to $8.95B ± $500M and non-GAAP EPS of $3.36 ± $0.20.
Consensus sits at $9.18B and $3.45.

The street is 2.6% above the company's own midpoint. Which means Applied can hit its own guide, print +23% YoY EPS growth, and still be reported as a miss. Watch which number the headline uses.

3. The base it's building from is already historically strong.

Q2 FY26: record revenue $7.91B, +11% YoY. Record non-GAAP EPS $2.86, +20%. DRAM revenue $1.7B, +18%, a record — driven by share gains in wiring, patterning and peripheral logic steps.

And the number that matters most: non-GAAP gross margin of 50.0% — the highest in more than 25 years.

That is not a cyclical company margin. That is a company with pricing power, in a market structure that grants it.

4. Which brings up the part nobody frames correctly: export controls are reshaping market structure, not just revenue.

The costs are real and quantified. China was 27% of Q2 FY26 sales (28% of fiscal 2025). Applied has flagged a $600M revenue hit in fiscal 2026 from broader curbs, including $110M in the October quarter. Commerce widened the entity list to cover majority-owned subsidiaries of listed firms. Applied separately paid $252.5M to settle export violations tied to roughly $1.4B of tools shipped to SMIC between 2016 and 2020.

Everyone reads that as a headwind. It is. But read the second-order effect.

Bain's structure-conduct-performance framework — the spine of industrial organization theory, and the reason concentration ratios get studied at all (Rui Mingjie, Industrial Economics, ch. on market structure, p.353/376) — says pricing power follows from structure, not from effort.

US export policy is doing something to this industry that no competitor could do: it is raising the entry barrier around a market and freezing the concentration of the suppliers permitted to serve the rest of it.

Applied gave up part of a 27% market. What it got back is a more defensible position across the other 73% — at a moment when that 73% is the only place AI capacity can physically be built. A 50% gross margin, the best in a quarter century, is what that trade looks like on the P&L.

I'm not arguing the policy is good or that the $600M doesn't hurt. I'm arguing the net structural effect runs in the opposite direction from the headline.

5. The hedge nobody talks about.

In April 2026, Applied took a 9% stake in BESI, becoming its largest shareholder. BESI is a central player in hybrid bonding — the process that stacks HBM and enables 3D logic, and which BESI expects to see sharply higher orders for through 2026–2027 on HBM4 and advanced logic roadmaps.

Applied's own hybrid bonding platform already sits in TSMC and ASE flows for 3D IC and HBM stacking, and is compatible with CoWoS and SoIC.

So: build it internally and own 9% of the main external competitor. Dixit and Nalebuff would call the equity stake a commitment device. I'd call it something simpler — Applied does not know which packaging architecture wins, and has arranged to be paid either way.

6. The open question I'd actually ask management tonight.

If wafer-scale spreads — if Cerebras's approach of trading silicon area for supply chain position gets copied — then HBM and CoWoS tool demand falls at the margin.

But wafer-scale still needs deposition, etch, metrology and wafer-level packaging. Applied plausibly sells into that outcome too.

Is Applied genuinely architecture-agnostic, or does it have a large book of revenue that only exists if the industry keeps stacking HBM? Nobody asks this question, and it's the only one whose answer changes the terminal value.

7. Five things to score tonight:

1. Revenue against $9.18B, not $8.95B. The gap between the company's guide and the street is where the headline gets written.
2. China as a % of sales. 27% last quarter. A sharp drop confirms the $600M is landing early; stability means the curbs are being absorbed.
3. Advanced packaging growth. Management has committed to >50% in calendar 2026. This is the single line most tied to AI, and the most checkable.
4. Non-GAAP gross margin holding 50%. A 25-year high is a hard thing to defend. Slippage here matters more than a revenue miss.
5. The first real FY2027 WFE framing. Applied says 2027 looks like 2026. Any hedging on that sentence is the story.
