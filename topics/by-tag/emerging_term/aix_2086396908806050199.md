🪂【SKYDIVING】
IPC vs. IPD vs. IVR: AI Chips at the Tactical Edge

At first glance, the AI compute race is about GPU, TPU, Trainium, Maia, and other AI accelerators competing on architecture and performance. But look deeper, and it becomes a battle shaped by the interaction of power integrity, signal integrity, and advanced packaging.

Ferric points out that modern compute and networking chips are consuming increasingly more power, while core voltages remain at only around 0.8–1.2 V. As a result, individual power rails can require thousands of amps of current, placing enormous pressure on power delivery at both the PCB and package levels.

The conventional approach—placing voltage regulators on the PCB and delivering power laterally through power planes and vias—begins to encounter significant challenges at high current densities, including higher power delivery network (PDN) impedance, weaker transient response, and efficiency losses.

At this level of power delivery, seemingly minor differences can translate into major system-level consequences.

—

▍IPC: Hidden in Plain Sight — Inside the Interposer

IPC and IPD are based on similar silicon capacitor technologies, but differ in their use cases, integration methods, and packaging formats.

IPC is essentially a large-area silicon interposer incorporating silicon capacitors. In a 2.5D advanced package, it carries the SoC and HBM, serves as the high-speed communication bridge between chips, and is customized according to the requirements of the system chip. IPC is typically delivered in wafer form.

In the past, this type of 2.5D packaging was limited to devices with a package integration area of up to approximately 4 reticle sizes.

As AI systems continue to scale, data traffic between ASICs and HBM is becoming increasingly intense—almost like thousands of horses charging simultaneously. The faster the data moves, the more difficult it becomes to maintain both signal integrity and power integrity.

By integrating silicon capacitors directly into the interposer, IPC effectively places a “power supply depot” immediately next to the battlefield. Power buffering is brought closer to the load, providing stronger support for system stability and performance.

—

▍IPD: Distributed Deployment — Hidden Within the Substrate

IPD takes a more distributed approach.

According to AP Memory, IPD is a discrete silicon capacitor device whose area, thickness, and placement can be adjusted depending on the application. It can be embedded directly inside the package substrate or placed externally on the substrate, and is typically delivered as individually packaged components.

When a 2.5D package needs to expand beyond the practical area limits of a conventional silicon interposer, a molding-based interposer becomes another option. In such architectures, redistribution layers are used to interconnect SoCs, memory devices, and other components, which are then integrated with molding materials.

Within this architecture, IPDs can serve either as embedded-in-substrate decoupling components or as externally mounted decoupling capacitors.

—

▍IVR: When Remote Power Delivery Is No Longer Good Enough

An Integrated Voltage Regulator (IVR) plays a fundamentally different role.

Whereas IPC and IPD primarily address capacitance, decoupling, and package-level power stability, IVR focuses on point-of-load power conversion—bringing voltage regulation physically closer to where the power is consumed.

By integrating technologies such as capacitors, magnetic thin-film inductors, and FinFET PMIC processes, the IVR consolidates voltage stabilization functions—which previously required dozens or even hundreds of components—into a miniature, series-connectable chip measuring just 40 µm², according to Raytek Semiconductor, the packaging provider for Ferric's IVR.

Ferric argues that as current requirements continue to rise, simply deploying larger regulators, thicker copper, or more decoupling capacitors may temporarily improve power delivery, but at the cost of board area and system cost, while delivering diminishing returns.

As power density continues to increase, PDN inductance and power-conversion losses increasingly become the dominant constraints.

The IVR approach is therefore to move voltage regulation closer to the load, shortening the power-delivery path and reducing impedance. For example, Ferric states that its solution can deliver more than 160 A from a 35 mm² package, corresponding to a current density of approximately 4.5 A/mm².

The key advantage of IVR is that power conversion occurs much closer to the point where current is actually consumed. By shortening the PDN and reducing parasitic inductance, IVR can improve both transient response and end-to-end power efficiency.

—

▍A TSMC History Lesson

Rewind to 2016, and IPD was already beginning to establish itself in the premium consumer electronics supply chain. At the time, TSMC had begun supplying IPD components associated with Apple's A10 processor for the iPhone 7.

At the core of the IPD structure is a silicon substrate etched with deep trench structures of approximately 30 μm in depth, with each trench having a pitch of roughly 2 μm.

These high-aspect-ratio trenches provide the structural foundation for high-density thin-film 3D integrated passive devices.

Multiple conductive layers—such as polysilicon and copper (Cu)—and dielectric materials—including SiO₂ and Si₃N₄—are subsequently deposited within the trenches. By repeatedly alternating conductive and dielectric layers, manufacturers can create a vertically arranged 3D structure with very high capacitor density.

Compared with traditional passive-component solutions used in high-frequency applications, including LTCC (Low-Temperature Co-Fired Ceramics) and SMD (Surface-Mount Device) components, IPD offers several advantages, including:

- smaller component size,
- thinner form factor,
- lower dielectric loss at high frequencies,
- higher integration density,
- and favorable thermal characteristics.

IPC and IPD, therefore, are not technologies that suddenly appeared with the emergence of AI.

They were already proven during the smartphone industry's long-term transition toward miniaturization, higher operating frequencies, and thinner form factors.
As 5G smartphone penetration increased, IPDs gradually replaced or supplemented certain conventional passive components because of their compact size, high level of integration, and strong high-frequency characteristics.

IPC can be viewed as the next evolution of this same design philosophy: rather than deploying silicon capacitors as discrete devices, the capacitors are integrated directly into the interposer, placing them even closer to the compute die while reducing the physical footprint of the overall power-delivery architecture.

In the AI era, what was once primarily a solution for saving space in smartphones is increasingly becoming a critical technology for delivering stable power to some of the world's most power-hungry chips.
