## 原文

The potential use case is especially interesting for inference. Large models require huge amounts of memory to store weights, but not every byte needs the same latency as the working data inside HBM. A system that keeps the most latency-sensitive data in HBM while placing a much larger model-weight pool in HBF could reduce cost and increase capacity.

Sandisk has published simulations suggesting that an HBF-supported system could perform close to an unlimited-HBM configuration for a large language model workload while offering 512GB per 16-die stack. That is promising, but it remains a company simulation rather than proof of commercial deployment. 

HBF should therefore be valued as long-term optionality, not current earnings.

The bull case is transformational. Sandisk could move from supplying storage behind the server to supplying a new memory tier directly beside the AI accelerator. That would increase strategic relevance, improve product differentiation, and potentially support margins above traditional NAND levels.

The bear case is equally clear. Technical standards do not guarantee adoption. HBF still needs working silicon, acceptable latency, strong endurance, packaging partners, software support, customer qualification, high manufacturing yields, and a compelling total cost of ownership. HBM vendors, CXL memory solutions, and future DRAM architectures will not stand still.

The current $SNDK valuation should not depend on HBF succeeding, but HBF can become a major source of upside if the technology moves from samples to named customer programs.

SANDISK IS NOT THE NAND MARKET LEADER

In calendar Q1 2026, Samsung held approximately 31.6% of global NAND revenue, SK hynix Group held 17.6%, and Sandisk, Kioxia, and Micron each held about 13.9%. Sandisk is therefore a major global supplier, but it is not the largest producer. 

That distinction matters because scale affects manufacturing costs, customer relationships, research spending, and the ability to survive downturns. Samsung can combine NAND, DRAM, HBM, logic, and foundry capabilities. SK hynix owns both HBM leadership and Solidigm’s enterprise SSD platform. Micron can offer NAND and DRAM while benefiting from U.S. industrial policy. Kioxia shares technology and manufacturing with Sandisk but remains a competitor in the end market.

Sandisk’s advantage is focus. Management can allocate capital, engineering, and sales resources entirely around flash and storage rather than balancing multiple memory businesses. The consumer brand, controller expertise, enterprise SSD roadmap, Kioxia manufacturing relationship, and HBF initiative create differentiation beyond raw wafer output.

Its disadvantage is the same focus. When NAND pricing collapses, Sandisk does not have a DRAM or HBM business to offset the decline.

THE 2026 SHORTAGE IS REAL

NAND supply remained severely constrained in 2026. Major manufacturers added very little new capacity, while AI-server demand, enterprise SSD adoption, and customer restocking pushed pricing sharply higher. TrendForce estimated that NAND could remain in a 4% to 5% supply deficit during 2026. 

This is why Sandisk can raise average selling prices by hundreds of percent while overall bit shipments remain flat. Customers are competing for limited supply, and producers have regained exceptional pricing power after years of weak industry economics.

The shortage also explains the long-term customer agreements. Hyperscalers and device manufacturers no longer want to depend entirely on quarterly negotiations when critical storage supply can constrain product launches. They are willing to make advances and commit future purchases to secure capacity.

The danger is that shortages create the conditions for their own end.
