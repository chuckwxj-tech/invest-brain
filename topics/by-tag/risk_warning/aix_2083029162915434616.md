AI Data Centers Are Eating Into $AAPL ’s Supply Chain

This is the most profound signal hidden in Apple’s latest earnings report.

Combined capital expenditure of $MSFT, $META , $GOOGL and $AMZN reached $725 billion this year, surging 77 % year‑on‑year. By contrast, Apple does not build AI data centers. Its capital expenditure over nine months stood at only $6.8 billion, down 28 % year‑on‑year.

Yet Apple cannot escape cost spillover from the AI infrastructure boom. Cloud providers demand training and inference chips, while Apple needs terminal‑side processors and memory chips. Though their end products differ, both compete for manufacturing capacity from the same group of suppliers.

First bottleneck: TSMC’s advanced manufacturing processes. Apple’s custom‑designed SoCs rely on cutting‑edge nodes including N3. All N3 production capacity was fully booked in 2026, with roughly 60 % allocated to AI‑chip customers; this share is projected to rise to 86 % in 2027. AI chips are crowding out production for consumer electronics. Tim Cook confirmed on the earnings call that supply constraints in the June quarter were concentrated on Mac, and will spread to iPhone and iPad in the September quarter. He attributed the issue to “faster‑than‑expected demand growth”. While AI was not explicitly mentioned, the industrial chain clearly knows where the production capacity has gone.

Second bottleneck: DRAM memory. Contract prices of DRAM jumped by more than 90 % in the first quarter of this year, with Goldman Sachs forecasting full‑year price gains between 250 % and 280 %. The root cause is straightforward: explosive demand for HBM (high‑bandwidth memory) used by AI servers. Samsung, SK Hynix and Micron are prioritizing HBM production, as HBM delivers 3‑to‑5‑times higher profit margins than consumer‑grade DRAM. Production capacity for consumer‑grade DRAM has been squeezed, driving up its prices.

Cook described the rally in memory prices as a “once‑in‑a‑century flood”. Memory costs were higher in the June quarter than the March quarter, which in turn exceeded the December quarter of last year, and are set to rise further in the September quarter. Apple was forced to reluctantly raise prices for multiple Mac, iPad and home devices in June.

Inventory data confirms mounting cost pressure. Its balance sheet shows inventory nearly doubled from $5.7 billion to $11.1 billion, a 94 % increase. Part of the buildup reflects advance stockpiling against supply shortages, while rising component prices including DRAM also inflated inventory value. Even with unchanged production volume, a 15 % rise in memory cost per iPhone alone pushes up total inventory value.

The contrast between $725 billion and $6.8 billion lays bare Apple’s predicament: it has no choice but to foot the bill for AI infrastructure expansion, albeit indirectly. Higher costs are passed into Apple’s gross profit margins through two channels: advanced chips and memory chips.

Tim Cook’s Last‑Ditch Move

Cook disclosed on the earnings call that Apple is “evaluating all options” to diversify DRAM supply sources.

Market reports point to $CXMT (ChangXin Memory Technologies), China’s DRAM maker. Cook indirectly commented: “The DRAM market has three major suppliers; more players would be beneficial.” This hints at a fundamental shift in Apple’s supply‑chain strategy: expanding beyond its concentrated dependence on Samsung, SK Hynix and Micron to include additional suppliers such as Chinese memory manufacturers.

CXMT currently holds around 7.67 % of the global DRAM market, and is the only Chinese firm capable of large‑scale mass production of DRAM.

Cook declined to confirm whether Apple is testing CXMT’s DRAM products. Nevertheless, the phrasing “evaluating all options”, paired with the urgency of a “once‑in‑a‑century flood” in memory pricing, sends a clear‑cut market signal.
