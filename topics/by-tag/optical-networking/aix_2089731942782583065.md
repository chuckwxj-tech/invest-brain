## 原文

Yesterday, NVIDIA invested $1.5 billion in SB Energy, and a lot of people asked me what I think about it.

So here’s how I’m looking at it.

From my perspective, the most important part is that NVIDIA is starting to directly help solve the financing problem behind future GPU orders.

For the PORTS-Pike project in Ohio, OpenAI is expected to lock in roughly 8GW of IT capacity, while SB Energy will be responsible for building and operating the infrastructure.

The flywheel, as I see it, is becoming:
OpenAI provides long-term compute demand
→ SB Energy secures the land and power
→ NVIDIA provides GPUs, technology, and part of the credit support
→ Wall Street provides project financing
→ the data center actually gets built and comes online.

AI demand has been strong for a long time, but not every company has a balance sheet like Microsoft or Google.

Now NVIDIA is starting to use its own credit strength to reduce the financing friction around AI infrastructure. In a way, it is actively helping push future GPU orders forward.

OpenAI is signing a 20-year lease, and the full campus will require at least 10GW of new energy capacity to support an 8GW AI Factory.

That stretches demand across the entire AI hardware stack.

Layer 1: GPUs
An 8GW-scale AI factory means multiple generations of GPU upgrades over time.

Today it may be Blackwell. Later it could be Rubin and whatever comes next.

NVIDIA gets paid not only from the initial deployment, but potentially from multiple rounds of hardware refreshes and software revenue.

Layer 2: Memory
More GPUs actually coming online means more HBM, more server DRAM, and more data-center SSDs.

For $MU, I think the implication is pretty direct.

Customers are already starting to lock in compute capacity beyond 2028.

Layer 3: Optical networking
At 4.25GW, and potentially 8GW later, the most practical question is the same one we kept talking about last week:
How do you connect all of those GPUs?

Lasers, silicon photonics, DSPs, OCS, and CPO all directly affect GPU utilization.

Once the GPUs are secured, networking becomes the next problem that has to be solved.

Layer 4: Power and liquid cooling
Going forward, one of the most important things to watch in AI data centers is when the facility actually gets powered on.

Transformers, energy supply, grid access, and power infrastructure all become critical.

Layer 5 — and the one I think matters most: the financialization of AI infrastructure.
NVIDIA is now helping customers solve the entire chain:
Land
→ Power
→ Financing
→ GPUs
→ Compute coming online.

That has the potential to extend and amplify the entire AI CapEx cycle.

For Neoclouds like $NBIS and $CRWV, I also see this as broadly positive for the industry.

Once again, it shows that long-term compute demand is still there.

The scarce resource is not just demand itself — it’s who can turn power and GPUs into billable compute the fastest.

Of course, this model also introduces new risks.

As NVIDIA, customers, infrastructure developers, and financial institutions become more tightly connected, I think customer payment capacity and project leverage become much more important things to watch.

Like I mentioned earlier, this week I’m still putting more of my attention on semiconductor equipment because I think the visibility there is currently stronger.

But once this part of the cycle plays out, capital may rotate again into the five layers I mentioned above.
