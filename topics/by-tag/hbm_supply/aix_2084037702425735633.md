## 原文

The AI chip everyone's buying can't ship without the one thing nobody is paying attention to: advanced packaging. Here's the whole board, and who actually captures the dollars. (Save this)

Advanced packaging is the assembly layer connecting multiple chips into one package that behaves like a single chip. For 50 years the industry made one chip on one piece of silicon (monolithic), and just wired it into a plastic case (traditional packaging). Advanced packaging uses several separate dies, a compute die, memory stacks (HBM), I/O dies, and fuses them together with ultra-dense wiring, or stacks them directly on top of each other.

I won't bore you with the details, but here's why it suddenly matters to the AI buildout. You physically can't print a chip big enough to do the job, so you build it from smaller dies and package them together. And without advanced packaging, HBM can't sit right next to the GPU, and without that, you can't build a modern AI accelerator. So to sum it up: if the chip is the brain, advanced packaging is how you wire many brains together into one super-brain without losing speed.

TSMC says its flagship 2.5D packaging platform, CoWoS, is booked through 2027. Lead times run 52 to 78 weeks, NVIDIA reportedly holds ~60% of capacity, and demand has roughly tripled to ~1M wafers in 2026. Both NVIDIA's and AMD's accelerators are CoWoS packaged to fuse the GPU with the HBM, so CoWoS capacity directly gates how many AI chips can ship, a bigger bottleneck than wafer capacity itself. That's the clearest proof yet that packaging, not lithography, is the true constraint.

But knowing packaging is the bottleneck is only half the trade. The other half is knowing who actually pockets the money when it gets this tight, and that's where most people stop looking. Because packaging isn't one business, it's a stack of four, and each layer does a completely different job at a completely different margin. You've got the chokepoint that owns the process, the assemblers that do the hands-on labor, the machine-makers that sell the tools doing the actual joining, and the eyes that inspect every bond before it ships. The same AI boom flows through all four but the dollars spent aren’t captured linearly. Here’s where the money really goes.

TSMC owns CoWoS outright and runs it in-house, which is why it can say the platform is sold out through 2027 and mean it. But owning the chokepoint cuts both ways, because TSMC is also the buyer everyone else in this story has to sell to, and that matters a lot when we get to who actually holds the pricing power.

One layer down sits the assemblers, the OSATs like Amkor and ASE that do the physical labor of putting the packages together, and this is exactly where most people get the trade wrong. Amkor grew revenue 25.6% last quarter, a real AI-packaging boom, and yet it still earns a 16.8% gross margin, guides $2.5 to 3 billion of capex, and carries no backlog at all. This signals high growth, but almost no capacity to collect perpetual rent. The assembler does the work and keeps very little of the money, which explains why for all the volume running through it, this layer is the trap, not the trade.

The real money sits one layer up in the machines. These are the companies that sell the bonding tools that do the actual joining, and this is where the next leg of the whole story lives: hybrid bonding, the shift to welding chips together copper-to-copper as solder runs out of room below ~10 micron pitch. Here's the part the crowd gets backwards. That shift is a logic story right now, driven by TSMC's SoIC and AMD's 3D V-Cache, not HBM4, which stays on microbumps until HBM5 around 2027. The cleanest way to see it is Besi, the hybrid-bonding pure-play, growing revenue 68.7% at a 65.7% gross margin with orders up 129% and AI already representing ~60% of its book, alongside Applied Materials and ASMPT selling into the same wave.

Then there's the layer almost nobody looks at, the eyes. This is inspection and metrology, the tools that check every bond before a package ships, and it's quietly the best seat on the board. One bad hybrid bond scraps an entire stacked die with no way to rework it, you can't sample anymore, you inspect 100%, and the dollars of inspection per wafer climb with every extra layer stacked, operating at ~55-61% gross margins. ONTO is the clean, US-listed company sitting at this checkpoint guiding advanced packaging revenue growth of ~30% this year.

Now lay one number over the entire board: gross margin. The assembler doing the labor earns ~17%. The machine-makers and the inspectors that sell and safeguard the tools earn ~55-66%. That gap is the pricing power addressed in real time. TSMC may own the chokepoint, but the tools it runs on come from two or three suppliers apiece, take years to qualify, and can't be swapped out mid-ramp, so even the most powerful buyer in the industry has to pay what the toolmakers ask. Pricing power sits with the tools, not the buyer and not the assembler. Same boom, and the dollars pool upstream every time. So the trade isn't "buy the packaging basket." It's to own the machine, not the assembler.

To conclude, everyone's still mapping the chip. The money is two layers upstream.
