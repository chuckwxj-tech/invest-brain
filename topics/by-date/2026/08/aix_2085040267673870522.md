$LITE $COHR $AAOI $NVDA PROPOSED FCC BAN ON CHINESE DATA-CENTER OPTICAL TRANSCEIVERS: INVESTMENT ANALYSIS

EXECUTIVE ASSESSMENT

The Counterpoint Research note is directionally correct in identifying the proposed FCC restriction as a potentially material redistribution of economics across the AI optical-transceiver supply chain. The proposal would be positive for qualified Western optical-module and laser suppliers, negative for Chinese transceiver leaders with high U.S. exposure, and incrementally adverse for hyperscalers through higher component prices, narrower supplier choice and potential deployment delays. However, the ultimate earnings impact is substantially more complex than a simple transfer of Chinese revenue to Coherent, Lumentum and Applied Optoelectronics. The decisive issues are the treatment of previously authorized products, the legal definition of a Chinese product, the availability of conditional approvals, the scope of covered components, hyperscaler qualification timelines and the amount of genuinely uncommitted Western capacity. As of August 5, 2026, the measure remains a reported draft that could be modified, delayed or abandoned, rather than an effective final rule. Reuters reported that the administration hopes to publish the restriction during 2026 and that the current concept focuses on new models of Chinese optical transceivers rather than requiring removal of the installed base. (Reuters)

The highest-conviction conclusion is that the proposed rule would create a higher-price, lower-supply-flexibility market rather than an immediate 1-for-1 substitution of Western modules for Chinese modules. Counterpoint estimates that Zhongji Innolight represents approximately 27 percent of global data-center optical-transceiver revenue, versus approximately 17 percent for Coherent and approximately 6 percent for Lumentum in finished modules. Coherent and Lumentum therefore represent only approximately 23 percent of module revenue in aggregate, below Innolight alone and well below the broader Chinese supplier base. Separate industry estimates cited by Reuters indicate that Chinese companies represented over 50 percent of optical-transceiver shipments during 2025. The arithmetic makes an immediate replacement of Chinese capacity physically implausible, particularly when existing Western capacity is already being consumed by rapid AI-network growth. (Counterpoint Research)

The most important temporal distinction is between the installed and already-authorized 800G ecosystem and the emerging 1.6T product cycle. FCC precedents involving routers, advanced robots and power inverters have generally allowed continued importation, marketing and use of previously authorized models while restricting authorization of new models. Conditional approvals have also been granted to individual suppliers and product classes. If the transceiver rule follows this architecture, the immediate 2026 effect would be inventory accumulation, last-time purchases and supplier-qualification activity rather than abrupt removal of Chinese 800G modules. The larger fundamental share shift would emerge during the 2027–2028 transition toward newly introduced 1.6T and eventually 3.2T designs. (FCC Docs)

The highest-quality public-equity expression is Coherent. Coherent combines the largest existing non-Chinese finished-module position, meaningful vertical integration in indium-phosphide lasers and other photonic components, active capacity expansion, and a $2 billion NVIDIA investment accompanied by purchase commitments and capacity rights. Lumentum is also a structural beneficiary, but its approximately 6 percent finished-module share understates its upstream laser exposure and simultaneously overstates the amount of Chinese module revenue that can become incremental Lumentum revenue. Some Lumentum lasers are already incorporated into modules assembled by Chinese suppliers; displacement of those customers can transfer module assembly economics without creating equivalent incremental laser demand. Applied Optoelectronics offers the highest event-driven operating leverage but carries substantially greater scale, customer-concentration, qualification and execution risk. Broadcom and Marvell are not clean beneficiaries because their merchant DSPs are already used by Chinese and Western transceiver manufacturers; supplier substitution largely relocates existing semiconductor content unless total optical deployments increase.

The proposed rule is modestly negative for Amazon, Microsoft, Alphabet and Meta at the direct cost level but potentially more consequential at the deployment-timing level. Optical transceivers represent a limited proportion of total AI-cluster capital expenditure, but an unavailable or unqualified transceiver can prevent activation of a much more valuable GPU, switch and network infrastructure stack. The relevant earnings transmission mechanism is therefore stranded or delayed AI capital, lower near-term cluster utilization and deferred cloud revenue rather than a material increase in depreciation from the optics bill of materials alone. NVIDIA has partially insulated itself through strategic investments and capacity-rights agreements with Coherent and Lumentum, potentially giving NVIDIA-linked systems better supply priority than hyperscalers procuring optics independently. The same shortage can still delay complete cluster activation and therefore remains a modest near-term risk to NVIDIA system throughput.

COUNTERPOINT’S CORE THESIS

Counterpoint correctly identifies 3 central consequences. The proposed ban would constrain U.S. hyperscaler deployment and increase costs, force Chinese suppliers to test whether Southeast Asian manufacturing can preserve market access, and improve pricing and strategic leverage for Western module and component suppliers that possess qualified products. Counterpoint also correctly emphasizes the difference between technical capability and available volume capacity. Coherent and Lumentum have advanced photonic designs, but design ownership does not create cleanroom, epitaxy, wafer-fabrication, optical-alignment, burn-in and test capacity immediately. Counterpoint’s observation that final assembly, optical alignment, testing and U.S.-designated shipping frequently occur in Southeast Asia is especially important because the economic consequences depend heavily on whether the FCC classifies products by manufacturing location, corporate ownership, control, technology provenance or some combination of those factors. (Counterpoint Research)

The note’s central weakness is insufficient separation of finished-module revenue from upstream component revenue. Optical-transceiver vendors occupy different points in the value chain. Coherent sells finished modules and a broad set of internal photonic components. Lumentum is particularly strong in high-value EML and continuous-wave laser technology while maintaining a materially smaller finished-module share. Broadcom and Marvell provide DSPs and related semiconductor content. Chinese suppliers perform substantial module design, packaging, optical alignment, firmware integration, assembly and test while often using selected Western chips or lasers. A restriction on Chinese finished modules therefore does not automatically create an equivalent increase in total addressable revenue for every Western component supplier. Some revenue is transferred, some is preserved through exports to non-U.S. markets, and some is delayed because substitute modules cannot be qualified or manufactured quickly enough.

The source also understates the significance of preexisting capacity commitments. NVIDIA agreed to invest $2 billion in Coherent and $2 billion in Lumentum, with multibillion-dollar purchase commitments and future capacity rights at each supplier. These transactions demonstrate that optical capacity was already becoming strategically scarce before the reported FCC initiative. They also mean that a meaningful portion of future Western capacity may already be economically committed. A hyperscaler losing access to Innolight or Eoptolink cannot assume that every incremental Coherent or Lumentum wafer is freely available. The policy can therefore increase supplier pricing and bargaining power without producing a proportionate near-term increase in delivered units. (Coherent Inc)

REGULATORY STRUCTURE AND POLICY PROBABILITY

Reuters reported that the contemplated mechanism would prohibit new Chinese optical-transceiver models while exempting many non-Chinese suppliers, and that the draft remains subject to modification or cancellation. The security rationale includes the possibility that compromised components could enable data theft, malware insertion or service disruption. The mechanism would extend an FCC strategy already applied to routers, drones, advanced robots and power inverters. The immediate market response reflected a relatively high perceived probability of implementation: Coherent rose approximately 11 percent, Lumentum approximately 7 percent and Applied Optoelectronics approximately 18 percent following the report. Those moves were event reactions rather than evidence that the final rule or economic outcome had been established. (Reuters)

The FCC’s recent actions indicate that the agency can pursue category-wide restrictions that initially cover foreign-produced equipment and then provide conditional approvals to suppliers that satisfy security, manufacturing or ownership requirements. The router and advanced-robot precedents explicitly preserved previously authorized models and applied restrictions principally to new models. The FCC has also distinguished production-location-based restrictions from producer- or provider-based restrictions. That distinction is the largest unresolved variable for Innolight and Eoptolink because manufacturing relocation to Thailand can address a country-of-production rule but cannot resolve a prohibition based on Chinese ownership, control or designation. (FCC Docs)

A narrow country-of-production rule would preserve a meaningful path for Chinese suppliers. Innolight, Eoptolink and other Chinese vendors could expand final assembly, testing and potentially optical-engine integration in Thailand or other jurisdictions. The effectiveness of this approach would depend on the amount of substantial transformation required, the origin of key subassemblies, the location of firmware loading and testing, and whether the FCC accepts the resulting product as non-Chinese. Eoptolink already has 2 Thailand sites, while Counterpoint describes Southeast Asia as an established location for assembly, alignment, testing and U.S.-allocated shipments. A narrowly drafted restriction could therefore accelerate geographic relocation without materially changing corporate market share. (Eoptolink)

An ownership- or control-based rule would be substantially more disruptive. Thailand assembly would not protect a product sold by a covered Chinese entity, and hyperscalers would need to qualify non-Chinese designs. That structure would produce the largest share gains for Coherent, Lumentum and Applied Optoelectronics, but it would also generate the largest supply deficit and greatest risk to AI-infrastructure deployment. A hybrid structure could prohibit designated Chinese entities while granting temporary approvals for specific products to prevent severe shortages. FCC precedents suggest that conditional and time-limited authorizations are credible tools rather than theoretical possibilities. (FCC Docs)

Policy certainty remains low. Reuters reported that Jefferies viewed implementation risk as limited and interpreted the proposal partly as negotiating leverage ahead of a planned U.S.-China leadership meeting and in response to Chinese rare-earth export controls affecting the U.S. optical industry. A similar Commerce Department effort was reportedly shelved earlier in 2026 after a period of trade détente. However, the FCC’s broader Covered List activity and the addition of Innolight to a Pentagon list of companies alleged to have Chinese military ties indicate that the proposal is consistent with a wider structural shift from restricting advanced technology exports to China toward restricting Chinese technology access to the U.S. market. The correct investment treatment is therefore a material but sub-100 percent policy probability, not dismissal as rhetoric and not capitalization of the full theoretical revenue transfer. (Reuters)

WHY THE NEW-MODEL DISTINCTION MATTERS

A restriction limited to new models would not create a conventional supply shock across the entire installed base. Existing 800G products that have already received authorization could continue to be imported and deployed, subject to the final language. Hyperscalers would have an incentive to increase purchases of approved Chinese designs before the effective date, extend existing hardware configurations and avoid product modifications that could require new authorization. Chinese vendors would have an incentive to preserve existing model identifiers and implement only changes that remain within permissible authorization parameters. The immediate result could be stronger Chinese 800G shipments and inventories before implementation, followed by a sharper transition problem when new 1.6T configurations require approval.

This dynamic could temporarily extend the 800G cycle. Hyperscalers unable to obtain sufficient qualified 1.6T modules could deploy additional 800G links, retain existing switch configurations longer or phase new clusters more gradually. That response would support 800G pricing and unit demand but reduce bandwidth density, increase optical-port count per unit of network capacity and worsen power efficiency relative to a timely 1.6T migration. It would also shift the effect upstream: demand for 224G electrical interfaces, next-generation switch silicon and 1.6T DSPs could be deferred even while aggregate demand for legacy 800G modules remains strong.

The policy is therefore more likely to change the slope and vendor composition of the 1.6T transition than to eliminate Chinese participation from the current 800G market immediately. The most consequential earnings revisions should emerge when hyperscalers finalize 2027 optical purchase plans, complete 1.6T qualifications and allocate volume across suppliers. Event-day share-price moves in 2026 can materially precede the corresponding revenue and gross-profit realization.

OPTICAL-TRANSCEIVER MARKET STRUCTURE

The relevant optical market is already operating under unusually strong demand and limited supply elasticity. Reuters Breakingviews cited a Bank of America forecast of approximately $26 billion for the market in 2026, versus less than $13 billion in 2025. It also cited estimates that supply deficits could reach approximately 60 percent for certain transceiver categories. These numbers indicate that the industry was moving into scarcity before any policy-driven removal of Chinese supply. (Reuters)

The bottleneck is distributed across the manufacturing chain. Additional module volume requires laser and photodiode wafers, DSPs, optical engines, fiber attach, precision alignment, thermal design, packaging, assembly, test, burn-in and reliability qualification. High-speed EML and continuous-wave laser capacity depends on specialized indium-phosphide epitaxy and wafer fabrication. Finished-module capacity also requires expensive test equipment and trained manufacturing labor. Moving final assembly from China to Thailand does not resolve a shortage of EML die, optical engines, cleanroom output or automated alignment capacity. Conversely, expanding wafer output does not create qualified finished modules unless packaging, firmware and customer-validation capacity expand in parallel.

Chinese manufacturers have historically supplied scale, engineering responsiveness and aggressive pricing. Removing or constraining over 50 percent of shipment capacity would reduce hyperscaler purchasing leverage and weaken the normal annual price-down dynamic. Qualified Western suppliers would likely capture higher average selling prices, better capacity-utilization economics and stronger contractual protections, including prepayments, purchase commitments and take-or-pay arrangements. The offset would be higher capital expenditure, increased working capital, expedited manufacturing costs and potentially elevated depreciation as new fabs and assembly lines enter service.

The gross-profit opportunity is therefore larger than the immediate unit-share opportunity. A supplier does not need to replace every prohibited Chinese unit to benefit materially. Higher utilization, scarcity pricing and reduced customer bargaining power can expand gross profit on existing volume. The reverse is true for hyperscalers: the largest economic cost may come from lower availability rather than the direct increase in module prices. AI infrastructure has low short-run demand elasticity, but deployment timing is highly sensitive to the availability of every critical subsystem.

TECHNICAL QUALIFICATION AND SUBSTITUTION RISK

Optical transceivers are not fully interchangeable commodities at the speeds relevant to AI clusters. A nominally standards-compliant 800G or 1.6T module must still satisfy the customer’s thermal envelope, host electrical interface, signal-integrity requirements, firmware behavior, management-interface implementation, forward-error-correction performance, link-budget assumptions, reliability standards and interoperability requirements with switches, NICs and cabling. Qualification can identify differences in power consumption, heat dissipation, error rates, receiver sensitivity, laser stability and firmware behavior that are not apparent from headline specifications.

A hyperscaler can accelerate qualification by allocating engineering resources, accepting selected specification compromises and financing supplier capacity. It cannot eliminate reliability testing or large-scale interoperability risk without increasing the probability of network failures. Substitution timelines are therefore more likely to be measured in quarters than in weeks. The policy burden will be highest for newly introduced 1.6T products, where the product ecosystem is less mature and qualification is occurring concurrently with manufacturing ramp.

The national-security rationale also creates differentiated risk by component type. A complete pluggable module contains active electronics, firmware, memory and management functions in addition to optical components. A laser chip or passive optical component has a materially different attack surface from a finished module containing DSPs and programmable control logic. A narrowly targeted rule could therefore prohibit complete Chinese modules while allowing U.S. laser and DSP suppliers to continue selling components to Chinese manufacturers for non-U.S. markets. A broader component rule would be much more disruptive and could affect Chinese-sourced subassemblies embedded in nominally Western products.

CPO and LPO are not immediate substitutes for constrained pluggable capacity. Co-packaged optics can reduce electrical reach and power consumption, while linear pluggable optics can reduce DSP content, but each architecture introduces separate thermal, serviceability, yield, control and qualification challenges. Each architecture also continues to require lasers, photonic components, packaging and test capacity. A prolonged restriction could accelerate architectural investment, but it would not solve a 2026–2027 transceiver shortage rapidly.

HYPERSCALER AND AI-INFRASTRUCTURE IMPACT

Counterpoint correctly identifies Amazon Web Services, Microsoft, Meta and Google as the principal downstream companies exposed to deployment bottlenecks and cost pressure. These companies consume optical modules at very large scale and have historically used multiple suppliers to improve availability, maintain price competition and reduce product-specific risk. Removing Chinese vendors from new-model procurement would reduce the qualified supplier set and shift negotiating leverage toward Western vendors. (Counterpoint Research)

The direct increase in optical spending would remain manageable relative to aggregate hyperscaler capital expenditure. The more material risk is that insufficient transceiver availability delays the activation of GPU clusters, Ethernet fabrics and scale-out networks. A module shortage can leave GPU servers, switches, cabling, power infrastructure and data-center capacity installed but not revenue-generating. The economic loss includes deferred cloud revenue, lower utilization of depreciating assets and a longer period before the project generates an acceptable return on invested capital.

The effect will not be uniform across the hyperscalers. Relative exposure depends on current Chinese module penetration, internal qualification resources, contractual access to Western capacity, willingness to accept higher prices, network architecture and the timing of the 1.6T transition. These procurement details are not disclosed with sufficient precision to support a high-confidence ranking among Amazon, Microsoft, Alphabet and Meta. Any claim that a particular hyperscaler is definitively most exposed would exceed the available evidence.

The likely near-term response is higher safety inventory, accelerated dual-source qualification, direct financing of capacity and longer-duration supply agreements. These measures will reduce disruption risk but increase working capital and weaken annual price negotiations. Hyperscalers may also standardize around fewer module designs to reduce qualification complexity. That standardization would benefit the selected suppliers but increase concentration risk and raise the cost of changing vendors later.

The proposed rule could also affect the cadence of AI capital expenditure reporting. A delayed optical subsystem does not necessarily reduce announced capital expenditure immediately because construction, GPU procurement and electrical infrastructure can continue. Reuters Breakingviews noted that approximately 60 percent of data-center projects expected for 2027 had not yet broken ground, creating substantial schedule flexibility and making it difficult to distinguish an optics-driven delay from power, construction, permitting or broader project-timing constraints. (Reuters)
