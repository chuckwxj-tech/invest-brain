INTEGRATION ROADMAP: 2D, 2.5D, AND 3D

The 2D configuration represents the lowest-complexity integration path. Compute, switching, memory, and optical-engine components are positioned side by side on a substrate or board. This approach preserves component modularity, facilitates known-good-die testing, and reduces thermal coupling. The disadvantages are longer electrical paths, lower bandwidth density, greater package-perimeter pressure, and potentially greater power. Near-packaged optics can be viewed as an intermediate form in which optical engines move closer to the ASIC without becoming fully integrated into the primary package.

The 2.5D architecture places logic, memory, and optical engines on a common interposer or advanced substrate. The shorter and denser electrical connections improve bandwidth and energy efficiency while preserving more physical separation than vertical stacking. This is likely to represent the most practical medium-term compromise because the semiconductor industry already uses 2.5D packaging extensively for AI accelerators and HBM. The incremental challenge is integrating photonic dies, fiber attach, external laser delivery, optical test, and thermal management into a package already constrained by large logic dies and multiple HBM stacks.

The 3D architecture offers the highest potential bandwidth density. Electronic and photonic functions can be vertically integrated, allowing optical I/O to emerge from locations across the die or interposer rather than being restricted to the package perimeter. This can free scarce die-edge area for additional HBM, electrical interfaces, or other chiplets. Marvell explicitly argues that vertically co-packaged photonics can free die-edge area and permit greater HBM content. Lightmatter’s 114 Tb/s active-photonic-interposer platform illustrates the conceptual potential of distributing optical connectivity across a multi-reticle area. 

The 3D configuration also creates the most severe manufacturing risk. Thermal gradients from high-power XPUs and HBM can shift photonic device behavior, particularly for wavelength-sensitive components. Vertical integration increases warpage, stress, alignment, power-delivery, cooling, and test complexity. Package yield becomes the product of logic-die yield, HBM yield, photonic-die yield, interconnect yield, fiber-attach yield, and final assembly yield. Rework becomes more difficult as component value and integration depth increase. A technically superior architecture can therefore remain economically inferior if compound yield or field reliability is inadequate.

The reference to microLED-based massively parallel optical interconnects should be treated as an exploratory research direction rather than a product roadmap. MicroLED arrays could theoretically provide highly parallel emitters, attractive spatial density, and alternative integration methods. Material transfer, emitter uniformity, modulation performance, coupling efficiency, thermal behavior, lifetime, control circuitry, and manufacturing yield remain material challenges. The microLED reference broadens the research agenda but has little near-term earnings relevance. 

THE STRATEGIC IMPORTANCE OF OPTICAL MEMORY

Extending optics to the memory interface is the most consequential proposal because it could decouple memory capacity from the physical perimeter and packaging constraints of an accelerator. Current high-performance systems place HBM adjacent to the XPU because electrical signaling cannot maintain comparable bandwidth and power efficiency over long distances. The available package area, interposer size, I/O shoreline, power delivery, and thermal envelope therefore limit HBM stack count and capacity. Optical interfaces could allow additional memory resources to be positioned farther from the accelerator while retaining substantially greater bandwidth and lower energy than conventional remote-memory connections.

The resulting architecture would not consist of a flat, uniform memory pool. A tiered hierarchy would remain necessary. On-die SRAM and cache would serve the most latency-sensitive data. Local HBM would hold the active high-bandwidth working set. Pooled DRAM or HBM-backed remote memory could hold warm data requiring greater capacity but tolerating higher latency. HBF could provide a higher-capacity NAND-based tier with bandwidth materially above conventional SSDs. Enterprise SSDs would retain the largest and slowest persistent tier. Software, memory controllers, compilers, runtimes, operating systems, and workload schedulers would need to place and migrate data across those tiers.

SK hynix’s broader product strategy is unusually aligned with that architecture. The company has presented a full-stack portfolio spanning HBM, CXL-based pooled memory, DRAM, HBF, and enterprise SSDs. Its HBF specification targets up to 512 GB and approximately 0.4-3.0 TB/s while using UCIe as the processor interface. SK hynix has also demonstrated CXL pooled-memory systems that transfer or reuse KV caches among servers and GPUs. CPO could eventually extend the reach and bandwidth of that pooled-memory concept beyond what electrical CXL links can economically support. 

The initial high-value workload is likely to be inference. Long-context models, reasoning systems, retrieval-augmented generation, and AI agents produce large KV caches that must remain accessible across multiple decoding steps. Storing every KV cache in local HBM is expensive and can force infrastructure operators to purchase accelerators for memory capacity rather than compute demand. Moving KV cache to SSD introduces substantial latency and can reduce token-generation performance. A shared, optically connected DRAM or HBM-cache tier could improve capacity utilization while preserving lower latency than storage.

Other potential workloads include embedding tables, recommendation-system features, model weights, inactive mixture-of-experts parameters, checkpoint data, retrieval indexes, and shared context across inference replicas. The value is greatest where data is large, reusable, irregularly allocated across accelerators, and less latency-sensitive than the active tensor working set. The value is lower where every memory access lies on the critical compute path and requires local-HBM latency.

Memory pooling could also reduce stranded capacity. Current systems statically attach memory to individual accelerators or servers. A workload with insufficient memory on an individual node cannot necessarily use idle memory elsewhere without costly data movement or software intervention. A coherent or software-managed shared pool could allow memory capacity to be allocated independently from compute. This improves utilization and potentially reduces the amount of memory and compute hardware required to meet peak workload demand.

Marvell’s emerging Photonic Fabric Memory Module provides a useful commercial analogue. The company describes a module supporting up to 8 DDR5 DIMMs, 72 GB of HBM cache, 7.2 Tb/s of optical-fabric bandwidth, connectivity across distances up to 30 meters, and less than approximately 350 ns of near-NUMA memory access. The associated interface uses CXL 3.1 and PCIe Gen 6. These remain company performance claims and forward-looking product disclosures, but they demonstrate that optical shared memory has progressed beyond academic discussion into system-level product development. 

HBM COMPLEMENTARITY AND CANNIBALIZATION

The base-case conclusion is that optical pooled memory will complement rather than replace HBM. Local HBM remains structurally advantaged for latency-sensitive, high-bandwidth, frequently accessed data. Optical links add electro-optical conversion, propagation, switching, protocol, coherence, and remote-controller latency. Even highly optimized optical memory will not reproduce the latency or energy characteristics of a wide, short, on-package HBM interface. Accelerator packages will therefore continue to require a substantial local HBM tier.

Optical I/O could support higher HBM content in certain architectures. Electrical I/O consumes package perimeter and die-edge area. Moving external communication to vertically integrated photonic interfaces can free physical space for additional HBM stacks or wider memory interfaces. Marvell explicitly identifies additional HBM capacity as a potential benefit of freeing die-edge connectivity. Faster optical fabrics can also improve the utilization of local HBM by allowing data to move between accelerator packages more efficiently. 

The negative scenario is nevertheless material. Shared remote memory could reduce the amount of HBM capacity required per accelerator for workloads currently forced to keep large, relatively cold data sets locally. It could also reduce accelerator purchases made primarily to acquire additional attached HBM. If infrastructure operators can scale pooled memory independently, the ratio of memory capacity to accelerator compute could change substantially. Total HBM demand per deployed workload could therefore decline even if HBM remains essential for the active working set.

The net impact depends on elasticity. Lower infrastructure cost and improved utilization could stimulate additional AI inference demand, more than offsetting reduced memory or accelerator content per workload. Larger addressable memory pools could also enable models and context lengths that are currently uneconomic. The outcome is therefore unlikely to be simple cannibalization. A more plausible result is segmentation: premium local HBM for active data, pooled DRAM or HBM-cache memory for warm data, HBF for high-capacity bandwidth-sensitive data, and SSDs for cold or persistent data.

SK hynix is better positioned against the cannibalization risk than a memory supplier focused exclusively on HBM. The company can potentially monetize HBM, server DRAM, CXL memory modules, HBF, NAND, SSDs, custom base dies, and controllers across the hierarchy. The strategic objective should be to capture the orchestration layer and controller economics rather than merely supply interchangeable memory bits. If accelerator or fabric vendors control placement, protocols, and controllers, SK hynix may retain volume exposure but surrender a disproportionate share of system value.

STRATEGIC IMPLICATIONS FOR SK HYNIX

The roadmap signals that SK hynix expects the basis of memory competition to shift. HBM leadership currently depends on DRAM performance, TSV technology, stacking, thermals, packaging, yield, and close qualification with accelerator customers. HBM4 and subsequent generations add greater customization through logic base dies and tighter integration with customer processors. Optical memory extends that co-design requirement into controllers, protocols, photonics, package architecture, software, and system topology.

Participation at the architectural-definition stage can create customer intimacy. Memory interfaces, coherence mechanisms, cache policies, optical link characteristics, failure handling, and tiering algorithms must be considered during accelerator design rather than added after silicon completion. A memory vendor involved in those decisions can become more difficult to replace and may secure earlier visibility into customer roadmaps. This could strengthen SK hynix’s HBM franchise even before direct CPO revenue materializes.

The potential moat would derive from a combination of memory-device expertise, custom logic, packaging, controller IP, photonic integration, firmware, system validation, and customer co-design. No individual layer is likely to be sufficient. Optical components may become standardized, while DRAM and NAND remain cyclical. The most defensible position would be ownership of an integrated memory subsystem optimized for specific accelerator architectures and workloads.

The principal strategic risk is that control remains with NVIDIA, Broadcom, Marvell, hyperscalers, or foundry and packaging partners. NVIDIA can define a vertically integrated accelerator, HBM, switch, networking, software, and system architecture. Broadcom can combine custom XPUs, switching, SerDes, DSP, and CPO. Marvell is assembling custom silicon, scale-up switching, CXL, and optical-I/O capabilities. TSMC controls critical advanced-packaging and silicon-photonics process technology. SK hynix may contribute essential memory while capturing less of the architectural premium unless it develops proprietary controller and interface capabilities.

The absence of named photonics, foundry, OSAT, laser, connector, or XPU partners is therefore a major diligence gap. Commercial CPO requires a coordinated ecosystem spanning electronic integrated circuits, photonic integrated circuits, lasers, fiber attachment, connectors, packaging, test, cooling, system design, and software. SK hynix cannot independently manufacture every layer. The economic outcome will depend on partner selection, intellectual-property ownership, cost allocation, yield responsibility, and customer control.

The roadmap is nevertheless consistent with broader SK hynix strategy rather than opportunistic marketing. The company has publicly emphasized tiered memory, customer co-design, CXL pooling, HBF, and the minimization of data movement across HBM, DRAM, NAND, and SSDs. The CPO publication adds a long-term physical connectivity layer to that strategy. The coherence across disclosures increases the probability that system architecture will remain a sustained investment priority, although it does not establish successful execution. 

COMMERCIALIZATION STATUS AND PROBABLE ADOPTION SEQUENCE

Switch-level CPO represents the 1st commercialization phase. NVIDIA states that Spectrum-X Ethernet Photonics switches with 200 Gb/s SerDes are in production. Broadcom has introduced a 102.4 Tb/s Tomahawk 6 Davisson CPO switch with 16 optical engines operating at 6.4 Tb/s each and 200 Gb/s per link. Broadcom describes the product as its 3rd-generation CPO architecture, indicating that merchant-switch vendors have accumulated multiple development cycles even though broad hyperscale deployment remains early. 

XPU-level optical I/O represents the 2nd phase. Ayar Labs has announced an 8 Tb/s UCIe optical chiplet. Lightmatter has announced a 114 Tb/s photonic-interposer reference platform. Marvell’s Celestial AI technology targets 16 Tb/s per optical chiplet and co-packaging with custom XPUs and scale-up switches. These disclosures establish significant technical progress but do not demonstrate the same production maturity as switch CPO. XPU integration faces tighter latency, thermal, package, yield, and protocol constraints, while customers are less willing to risk failure of a high-value accelerator package. 

Optical shared-memory appliances represent the 3rd phase. Marvell has described a concrete architecture combining DDR5, HBM cache, CXL, and optical fabric, while SK hynix has demonstrated electrically connected CXL pooled-memory applications. Early systems are likely to use explicit software management, caching, and tiering rather than fully transparent hardware coherence. This reduces protocol complexity and permits workload-specific optimization, particularly for KV cache and inference. 

Photonic-interposer-based memory directly integrated into XPU packages represents the 4th and most uncertain phase. This architecture requires simultaneous maturity across optical devices, 3D packaging, fiber attachment, thermal stability, memory controllers, coherence, standards, software, test, and manufacturing economics. The probable adoption path is selective rather than uniform. High-value hyperscale systems with severe bandwidth and power constraints will adopt earlier, while enterprise and lower-density systems retain copper and pluggable optics for longer.

A probability-weighted timing framework places continued switch-CPO deployment in 2026-2028, selective XPU optical-I/O introductions and customer qualifications in approximately 2027-2029, and broader optical-memory deployment after successful system pilots. Marvell expects meaningful Celestial AI revenue in 2H FY2028, reaching a company-projected $500 million annualized run rate in Q4 FY2028 and $1 billion in Q4 FY2029. Those targets provide a useful industry marker but remain forward-looking and execution-sensitive. Broad photonic-memory-interposer adoption should be treated as a 2030-plus possibility rather than a base-case near-term forecast. 

MANUFACTURING, YIELD, AND SERVICEABILITY

Compound package yield is a central commercial risk. NVIDIA’s Spectrum-X package contains 32 photonic engines. As a purely illustrative calculation, assuming statistically independent yield of 99.5 percent for each engine integration step would produce only approximately 85.2 percent aggregate yield across 32 engines. Improving each step to 99.9 percent would raise aggregate yield to approximately 96.8 percent. These figures are not NVIDIA yield estimates; they demonstrate why known-good-die testing, reworkability, automated fiber attachment, process control, and defect isolation are economically critical in CPO. 

Testing must occur across electrical and optical domains. Photonic engines require characterization of insertion loss, wavelength behavior, modulator performance, receiver sensitivity, laser coupling, thermal drift, bit-error rate, and aging. Testing a die before final assembly is difficult when full optical paths, external lasers, fiber connections, and neighboring electronics are not yet present. Testing only after assembly risks scrapping a package containing high-value switch or accelerator silicon and HBM.

Fiber attachment introduces additional precision requirements. High-density packages can contain 100s of optical channels. Misalignment, connector contamination, mechanical stress, thermal cycling, and packaging warpage can degrade coupling efficiency or reliability. Automated attachment is essential for volume economics. NVIDIA’s use of detachable optical connectors and its emphasis on manufacturing automation are evidence that fiber integration remains a core engineering problem rather than a commoditized process. 

Serviceability remains a structural disadvantage relative to pluggable optics. External laser modules improve maintainability because lasers are among the more failure-prone and temperature-sensitive optical components. NVIDIA states that its external-laser architecture reduces the laser count by approximately 4x and allows field replacement. However, co-packaged modulators, receivers, waveguides, electrical interfaces, and fiber connections remain embedded in the switch package. A failure can therefore disable ports or require replacement of a higher-value assembly. 

The operational economics depend on graceful degradation. A package that can continue operating with a failed optical lane or engine may remain commercially viable, while an architecture requiring replacement after an individual defect could be uneconomic. Redundant lasers, spare lanes, error correction, rerouting, telemetry, fault isolation, and modular connectors will therefore be as important as peak bandwidth.

THERMAL AND POWER RISKS

Photonics must operate adjacent to increasingly hot logic and memory. High-end XPUs, switches, voltage regulators, and HBM stacks create substantial local heat flux and temperature gradients. Resonant photonic devices can be sensitive to temperature because wavelength alignment shifts as materials heat. Thermal tuning consumes power, reducing the apparent energy advantage. External lasers can be placed in a controlled environment, but modulators, photodetectors, and associated electronics remain near the compute package.

CPO may improve total system power while worsening local package cooling. Power previously distributed across front-panel transceivers moves closer to the ASIC. The aggregate rack may consume less energy, but the package and cold plate must remove additional heat from a highly constrained area. Liquid cooling and advanced thermal interfaces are consequently likely to become more important.

The less-than-1-pJ/bit target should therefore be assessed alongside heat density, not only total energy. An optical engine with excellent energy per bit can still create a problematic hot spot when concentrated into a small package delivering more than 100 Tb/s. System-level performance depends on watts per square millimeter, coolant temperature, thermal resistance, wavelength stability, and throttling behavior.

PROTOCOL, COHERENCE, AND SOFTWARE RISKS

Optical connectivity addresses the physical link but does not by itself create usable shared memory. A pooled-memory system must define addressing, cache coherence, consistency, ordering, atomic operations, access control, error handling, congestion management, quality of service, isolation, and failure recovery. A link can provide extremely low device-level latency while the complete memory transaction remains constrained by protocol and controller overhead.

Full hardware coherence across 100s of accelerators may be impractical because coherence traffic and directory state can scale rapidly. Early optical-memory systems are therefore likely to use restricted coherence domains, software-managed memory, explicit data movement, caching, page migration, or workload-specific semantics. CXL provides a useful framework for memory expansion and pooling, but extending CXL-class semantics across multiple racks and optical fabrics requires careful control of latency, topology, and failure behavior.

Security and multi-tenancy are also material. Shared memory must prevent unauthorized access, preserve tenant isolation, encrypt or authenticate traffic where required, and securely invalidate data when resources are reassigned. Memory pooling increases the blast radius of controller or fabric failures. Hyperscalers will require telemetry, fault containment, observability, and deterministic recovery before deploying the architecture for mission-critical workloads.

Software placement may determine more value than the physical link. Effective systems must predict which data should reside in local HBM, remote DRAM, HBF, or SSD. Poor placement could create excessive remote accesses and erase the performance benefit. Compilers, inference runtimes, schedulers, cache managers, and application frameworks must become aware of bandwidth, latency, congestion, and memory cost. SK hynix’s ability to influence or provide that software layer will affect whether it captures system-level value.
